# Mitigating Cathodic Corrosion of Lead Electrodes via Electrolyte Additives and Alloying Strategies

## Abstract

The corrosion of electrodes remains a critical challenge in advancing organic electrosynthesis. While anodic corrosion has been extensively studied, cathodic corrosion remains poorly understood. Recent studies suggest that the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) may play a central role in driving cathodic corrosion, with two primary mechanisms proposed: (1) hydride formation at the metal surface and (2) mechanical stress induced by evolving hydrogen gas. In this study, we investigated the cathodic corrosion of lead (Pb) electrodes as they are widely employed in electro-organic reduction reactions due to their high HER overpotential but are notably susceptible to cathodic degradation. Specifically, we explored two distinct approaches to mitigate the cathodic corrosion of lead: suppression of HER through electrolyte modification, and structural reinforcement via metal alloying. The first approach consists in limiting the diffusion of protons/hydronium ions to the surface of the lead, thus suppressing HER. To this end, we tested various quaternary ammonium sulfate salt (QAS) additives for which the cations electrostatically interact with the surface of the negatively charged lead electrode, creating a cationic layer that protects the cathode’s surface by repelling hydroniums, thereby suppressing HER. Chronoamperometry (CA) at -1.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl (after the onset of HER) in 0.1 M H 2 SO 4 (pH 1) revealed that even low concentrations (1 μM) of QAS drastically reduced hydrogen evolution. Specifically, the evolved hydrogen dropped from ~1.62 mmol/cm 2 to ~0.29 mmol/cm 2 in the presence of 1 µM of N,N,N,N’,N’,N’-hexamethyl-1,12-dodecanediammonium methyl sulfate (H12MS)), the most effective QAS tested. In addition, CA data showed that the current increased over time for bare Pb electrodes, indicating a corrosion-induced increase of the surface area, whereas it remained stable in the presence of QAS, confirming corrosion mitigation. In addition, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) indicated improved charge transfer resistance due to the cationic species near the electrode surface. The combined effects of QAS on mass and charge transfer enhanced the faradaic efficiency (FE) of the electrochemical hydrogenation of fumaric acid to succinic acid used here as a test reaction. At -1.6 and -1.7 V vs. Ag/AgCl, FEs more than doubled in the presence of QAS despite similar product yields, indicating effective suppression of parasitic HER. The second approach consists in alloying Pb with metals that are less susceptible to cathodic corrosion. Alloyed electrodes were evaluated via CA, revealing significantly more stable currents compared to pure Pb, suggesting reduced pitting and overall improved corrosion resistance. These findings highlight alloying as a viable strategy for the structural stabilization of Pb electrodes. Together, these approaches demonstrate effective pathways for mitigating cathodic corrosion and enhancing FE for electrochemical reductions. The insights gained from this study inform the development of robust, non-precious cathode materials for sustainable electrosynthesis applications.
