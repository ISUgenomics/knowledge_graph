# Electrochemical Regeneration of Platinum Catalysts Poisoned by Sulfur-Containing Species

## Abstract

The integration of biomanufacturing and electrocatalysis provides a promising framework for advancing the decarbonization and electrification of the chemical industry. This approach leverages the microbial transformation of biomass into platform intermediates and their electrocatalytic diversification into value-added products directly within the fermentation broth [1,2]. Integrating these transformations through process intensification circumvents downstream separation and purification steps, thereby improving process efficiency and enhancing economic feasibility. Despite these advantages, the presence of sulfur- and nitrogen-containing biomolecules intrinsic to fermentation media introduces a major challenge, as these biogenic impurities are known to poison precious metal catalysts under thermocatalytic conditions. To address this challenge, we investigated the catalytic activity and deactivation kinetics of platinum (Pt) catalysts for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), used here as a model system. Using cyclic voltammetry (CV), we evaluated Pt activity in the absence and presence of various biogenic impurities, including sulfur-containing amino acids (such as cysteine and methionine), peptides (such as glutathione), and complex protein mixtures (such as peptone). Our findings reveal the pronounced susceptibility of Pt to poisoning and deactivation in the presence of these impurities, with 1000 ppm cysteine inducing an 80% decrease in HER activity due to strong thiol adsorption and subsequent blocking of active sites. Importantly, we demonstrate that this deactivation can be fully reversible under electrochemical conditions. Electrochemical regeneration via reductive stripping – applied using chronoamperometry (CA) at incrementally negative potentials interspersed with CVs – restored HER activity to pristine levels. Reductive stripping at -1.2 V vs. Ag/AgCl (KCl sat’d) yielded post-regeneration CV responses with peak currents comparable to those of pristine Pt. The adsorption and removal of cysteine were further confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), which showed the disappearance of surface-bound sulfur species following regeneration. In situ surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (SEIRAS) was employed to monitor the adsorption and desorption of thiol-containing species on Pt in real-time, providing direct spectroscopic evidence of impurity binding and its subsequent electrochemical removal. To assess the broader applicability of this regeneration strategy, we extended our study to the electrocatalytic hydrogenation (ECH) of fermentation-derived cis,cis -muconic acid ( cc MA) to adipic acid (AA), a key building block in nylon 6,6 production. In the presence of cysteine, Pt surfaces were completely deactivated, with no observable cc MA conversion. Remarkably, reductive stripping at -1.1 V successfully restored the lost catalytic activity, enabling 100% conversion to AA with product yields matching those obtained on pristine Pt. These experimental findings were further supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which elucidated the binding energetics of the biogenic species and confirmed the thermodynamic favorability of impurity desorption under reducing conditions. Overall, our findings demonstrate that under electrochemical conditions, poisoning of Pt catalysts by sulfur-containing biomolecules is reversible, paving the way for the direct use of fermentation broths in electrocatalytic transformations. By integrating fermentation and electrocatalysis, this work represents a significant step toward decarbonized and electrified routes for sustainable chemical manufacturing.
